The cloud
is made of concrete.

“The cloud” sounds weightless. It isn’t. It’s buildings full of chips, wired to power plants and plumbed into watersheds. Here’s what the research says about AI’s environmental footprint, from one prompt to the whole planet.

01

Where your prompt goes

The trip takes a second or two. Here’s everything it touches along the way.

  1. 1

    You hit send

    Your words leave your laptop as light pulses in fiber-optic cable, bound for a data center that may be hundreds of miles away.

  2. 2

    GPUs do the math

    Racks of specialized chips run the model, predicting your answer one token at a time. Nearly every watt they draw ends up as heat.

  3. 3

    Cooling fights the heat

    Chillers and evaporative cooling towers carry that heat away. In U.S.-average conditions about 0.55 L of water evaporates per kWh on site.

  4. 4

    The grid powers it all

    On the average U.S. grid, each kWh emits about 348 g of CO₂ and consumes another ~3.1 L of water at power plants.

  5. 5

    The answer comes back

    A typical text answer costs about 0.3 Wh. That’s tiny, until you multiply by every student, every retry, every image, every day.

The rebound effect

When something gets cheaper and faster to use, we tend to use more of it. Economists call this the Jevons paradox. Google cut the energy of its median prompt 33× in a year , yet data-center electricity worldwide is still on track to more than double by 2030 . Efficiency is necessary. On its own it isn’t enough.

Calculate my print Reflect: Stewardship

Stewardship · A Catholic Social Thought lens

Care for our
common home.

When Robert Prevost became Pope in May 2025, he chose the name Leo, recalling Leo XIII, who answered the first industrial revolution with Rerum Novarum (1891). A year later Leo XIV published his first encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas, on safeguarding the human person in the age of artificial intelligence. This page reads AI’s footprint through that tradition.

∞

See · Judge · Act

Developed by Cardinal Joseph Cardijn for the Young Christian Workers and endorsed by Pope John XXIII, this three-step method moves from facts, to principles, to practice.

SeeReview the concrete situation honestly, with data. JudgeWeigh it in the light of the Church’s social principles. ActDecide what can and should be done, then do it.
“These three stages are usually expressed in the three terms: look, judge, act.”John XXIII, Mater et Magistra §236

See: the footprint is real

The Church’s documents on AI name its material cost directly.

“Current AI models and the hardware required to support them consume vast amounts of energy and water, significantly contributing to CO₂ emissions and straining resources.”
Antiqua et Nova §96 (2025)

The same note warns that metaphors like “the cloud” can hide the physical infrastructure behind AI. Magnifica Humanitas asks that technology’s new possibilities be used to “respect the environment, avoid waste and prevent new forms of exploitation” (§67).

Judge: four permanent principles

The Church names four principles that hold together all of its social teaching: the dignity of the human person, the common good, subsidiarity, and solidarity. Tap each one to apply it to AI’s footprint.

Integral ecology: everything is connected

Laudato Si’ insists that environmental and social problems can’t be solved separately: “We are faced not with two separate crises, one environmental and the other social, but rather with one complex crisis which is both social and environmental” (§139). Magnifica Humanitas carries this into the AI era, joining “the cry of the earth and the cry of the poor” (§43). Hover or tap a node to trace its connections.

Stewardship: to till and to keep

“The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to till it and keep it.”Genesis 2:15

Laudato Si’ reads this verse as a charter: tilling means cultivating and working, and keeping means caring, protecting, overseeing and preserving (§67). Creation is entrusted, not owned.

Magnifica Humanitas calls scientific discoveries “talents entrusted to humanity so that they may bear fruit” (§9). In the parable, the servants are judged neither for owning talents nor for refusing to use them, but for what they did with them. AI is such a talent. Stewardship doesn’t ask students to bury it. It asks them to use it in a way that keeps the garden.

That rules out two easy answers: rejection (“never use AI”) and indifference (“it’s just electricity”). What’s left is the harder middle path: using a powerful tool deliberately, in proportion to the good it actually serves.

Act: from habits to institutions

Laudato Si’ praises small daily choices: “We must not think that these efforts are not going to change the world” (§212). But Magnifica Humanitas also gives schools, universities and local communities a voice in the choices that shape daily life (§71). Act on both levels.

    1. Ask for transparency. Encourage your university to publish the energy and water impact of the AI tools it licenses, and to choose providers that report it. Subsidiarity
    2. Shape campus AI policy. Student government and faculty senates can write sustainability into AI guidelines: default to efficient models, discourage wasteful use. Common good

    Method & Sources

    How we calculate your print

    Every factor below is drawn from a published study, company disclosure or government dataset. Where sources disagree, we picked a central estimate and say so.

    The model

    weekly energy (Wh) =
    chatbot prompts × [(1 − long share) × 0.3 + long share × 2.5] × model factor
  + reasoning prompts × 5.15
  + coding requests × 2.5
  + AI search answers × 0.24  +  in-app AI messages × 0.3
  + images × 1.35  +  video clips × 944

chatbot prompts = days per week × prompts per day × retry factor
yearly energy   = weekly energy × weeks of use
water (mL)      = energy (Wh) × 3.692     carbon (g CO₂) = energy (Wh) × 0.348

    Factors

    What’s included, and what isn’t

    Included

    • Electricity used by AI chips, host servers, idle capacity and data-center overhead (cooling, power conversion) per request
    • Water evaporated for cooling on site, plus water consumed to generate the electricity
    • Carbon from electricity at the U.S. average grid mix (location-based)

    Not included

    • Training the models (amortized over billions of requests, it’s small per prompt, but large in absolute terms)
    • Your own device and home internet
    • Manufacturing chips and buildings (embodied carbon, e-waste)
    • Companies’ renewable-energy purchases (market-based accounting would lower carbon)

    Honest caveats

    • Companies don’t publish per-request data for most products. Our figures come from a mix of first-party disclosures (Google, OpenAI) and independent benchmarks and estimates (Epoch AI, Jegham et al., MIT Technology Review). Real values vary by model, data center, time of day and response length.
    • Estimates have been falling. A widely-cited 2023 figure of ~3 Wh per ChatGPT query is now considered roughly 10× too high for typical text prompts. Hardware keeps getting more efficient.
    • Location matters a lot. The same prompt served from a hydro-powered region can emit a small fraction of the U.S. average carbon, while a hot, dry region can use far more water.
    • Survey ranges are approximations. Options like “3–4 days” use their midpoints. The “typical student” profile is illustrative, built from survey defaults informed by Lumina–Gallup usage rates, not a measured average.
